
Valdés-Sosa et al. (2000)](https://doi.org/10.1037/0096-1523.26.2.488) introduced a transparent-motion experimental design that has spawned numerous psychophophysical and neurophysiological studies (refs from Stoner and Blanc, and Çatak et al.).  Taken together, these studies appear to provide the best evidence for object-based attention (Stoner and Blanc, Çatak et al).  
As observed by Stoner and Blanc (this should link to paper) for the object-based account to be a valid, the attentional cues (whether endogenous, exogenous or both) in these experiments must first give an attentional advantage to the subset of overlapping dots rotating one direction and then to the same set of dots when they translate.  The visual system must therefore somehow "bind" the cued rotation direction with the translation in an object-specific manner.  The mechanisms underlying this type of binding have not been discovered nor have any directly applicable models beend advanced. 

Casting doubt on the object-based explanation, Stoner and Blanc suggested that the various results from these transparent-motion could be explained by established competitive interactions between direction selective neurons (e.g. refs).  According to this account, when multiple stimuli appear in the visual field they activate distinct populations of neurons that compete with each other. Which neurons win this competition can be biased by the strength of the stimuli (such as that dictated by luminance contrast) as well as by exogenously or endogenously-directed selective attention ([Luck, Chelazzi, Hillyard, & Desimone, 1997](https://doi.org/10.1152/jn.1997.77.1.24); [Moran & Desimone, 1985](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.4023713); and [Reynolds, Chelazzi, & Desimone, 1999](https://doi.org/10.1523/JNEUROSCI.19-05-01736.1999)). As described below, Stoner and Blanc devised a simple model based on competitive interactions between direction-selective neurons that was broadly consistent with established neurophysiological properties and could plausibly account for a wide variety of findings. This provided a serious challenge to a large body of research that had appeared to provide the best support for object-based attention.
