[Stoner & Blanc (2010)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.visres.2009.11.015) applied the competition model of [Reynolds, Chelazzi, & Desimone (1999)](https://doi.org/10.1523/JNEUROSCI.19-05-01736.1999) to the delayed-onset design ([Figure 1](#figure-1)) — the single-translation variant introduced by [Reynolds, Alborzian, & Stoner (2003)](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0042-6989%2802%2900403-0). That design carries a *motion-duration confound*: a cued translation occurs alongside the older, longer-present rotation, an uncued translation alongside the newer one. Cueing is therefore confounded with how long the surrounding rotation has been visible — a bias that requires no object-specific binding.

Two assumptions turn that confound into the cueing advantage. First, rotation-tuned neurons **adapt** — their response wanes the longer a direction has been present. Second, they **divisively suppress** a translation-tuned detector. Because a cued translation is accompanied by the older, more-adapted rotation, that rotation suppresses the detector less, and the cued translation evokes the larger response (see Stoner & Blanc, 2010). The figures below trace this computation and its sharp prediction: decouple duration from cueing with a *motion swap*, and the advantage should reverse.
