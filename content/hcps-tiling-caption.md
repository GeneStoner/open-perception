The tiling holds spacing proportional to σ, and it **staggers alternate rings by half the spacing**. **(B) The web, true positions.** The boxed wedge from A, with every point-set drawn at spacing/2 and the example point-set and its two neighbours — **the darker profiles in C** — drawn at **FWHM/2**, the extent convention used throughout, since a disc of that radius holds exactly half the drive. 49 point-sets shown. **(C) An arc section** along one ring, through the highlighted example (ring 11, E = 1.11°), in units of point-sets: **RF σ = 1.50 point-sets**, every centre hit exactly, so no height variation.

**Why an arc and not a radial cut.** An arc follows a single ring, where every point-set has the **same σ and the same phase**, so the path passes **exactly through every centre** — the same 7 point-sets marked in B. There is therefore no height variation to suppress and none to draw. A radial cut cannot do this: because the tiling staggers alternate rings, no radial line passes through every centre, and the best available line still runs **0.33σ** off the worst ring. That is the reason to prefer an arc.

**Every RF is drawn at unit peak, as the model uses them** — RF height does **not** vary with eccentricity. What varies is σ, and it varies in proportion to spacing: **σ/spacing = 1.504** along the arc and **1.453** radially, so the tiling is isotropic to about 3% and these ratios are constant at every one of the 19 rings. Panel C is therefore not a picture of ring 11 — it is the picture at **every eccentricity**.
